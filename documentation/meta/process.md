# process

**in short.** how the work gets done. its mechanics: how an idea goes from
noticed to documented, how drafts become chapters, how things get checked, and
how changes are kept and explained. and its dynamics: staying engaged first,
with attention moving, at a natural pace, between abstract and applied periods,
between ideas, and between the small structure and the large; following
tangents, and coming back; patience, over years if need be; and tying work off
into self-contained pieces. the docs are the product; this is the way they're
made.

the concepts here are listed, with stable IDs, in
[concepts.md](../concepts.md).

## mechanics

### the life of an idea

an idea goes through a few stages, and doesn't have to reach the end:

- **noticed**: it comes up while working on something else, and gets a note,
  often in the rough notes
- **explored**: it's talked through, tried on examples, and compared with
  what's established, before anything is written into the docs
- **decided**: a name and a shape are settled, along with the reasons, and
  what was rejected
- **documented**: it goes into a chapter, in dependency order, with its
  concepts in the index
- **checked**: its examples are recomputed, and it's read against everything
  around it
- **deferred**, at any stage: noted on purpose, and left for later

the order matters most at one point: an idea is explored before it's written
down. writing too early fixes a shape that hasn't been tested.

### drafts and revisions

the docs grow in drafts. the strand domain, first called spacer, began as one
rough draft; when it was ready to be shaped, it was frozen as a version, split
into chapters with its text unchanged, and then revised in the chapters. each
step was its own change, so each can be checked on its own: the split moved
text and nothing else, and the revision changed text and nothing else.

### open questions

questions that come up and can't be settled straight away are listed, then
worked through in order. each is either resolved, with the resolution
recorded, or deferred, with the reason. a question that turns out to be badly
posed is restated, not forced to an answer.

### consistency passes

**builds on:** domain, in "documents".

after a run of changes, the whole of a domain is read again, start to finish,
looking for contradictions, leftovers from earlier versions, and rough joins.
the findings are reported first, sorted into what needs a decision and what's
mechanical, and only then fixed. the first pass over the strand chapters found
a word used for two things ("view"), a definition too narrow for what it
covered ("operation"), and about a dozen leftovers.

### checking

worked examples are recomputed by script, not trusted. a check says how far it
went: how many cases, up to what size. a claim recalled from memory stays
labelled until it's checked (see "status labels", in "documents").

### keeping changes

every change is kept, with a message that says what changed and why. moves
and renames keep their history. a rename changes every use at once; the
concept keeps its ID, and its former name is recorded.

**the case against.** process can turn into ceremony, slowing the ideas down
to serve itself. it's there to make thinking checkable, and should stay as
light as that allows.

## dynamics

### engagement first

staying engaged with the work, and passionate about it, matters more than
forcing attention onto one narrow thing. attention is left to move at a
natural, unforced rate: when a scope starts to go stale, the work moves to
something else that still serves the same goal, and comes back when it's fresh
again. a goal broad enough to move within makes this possible: becoming
better, as a person and as a participant in society, can be pursued through
many domains and problems, and an open-ended field, such as software, is a
good vehicle for it (see [personal](personal.md)). the point is staying
engaged with improving, not the field it happens in. without strict deadlines,
the work keeps improving and evolving, rather than burning out or going stale.

the sections below are kinds of this movement, along different axes: between
abstract and applied periods, between ideas, and between the small structure
of the work and the large. the axes aren't the point; staying engaged is.

two counterweights keep engagement from turning into drift:

- **finishing**: every so often, a whole, self-contained piece of work is taken
  to completion, to keep the skill of finishing in practice
- **discomfort**: what's uncomfortable is practised on purpose, not only what's
  enjoyable

both are held with an eye on sustainability: the aim is to keep going for
years, not to win the week.

**the case against.** without deadlines, some things never finish, which is
what the first counterweight is for. engagement can slide into comfort, which
is what the second is for. and work that others depend on may need deadlines
anyway: this suits work whose pace can be set from the inside.

### abstract and applied, in turn

the work doesn't stay at one level. it moves back and forth between two kinds
of period:

- **abstract periods**: deep thought, reframing, models and perspectives: a new
  domain sketched out, a word pulled apart, a whole meta domain drafted in a
  day
- **applied periods**: putting those abstractions to work, building and
  shipping, and realizing economic or cultural value with them. sometimes that
  means setting an abstraction aside on purpose, because value now matters
  more than refining it further

this **oscillation** is how each kind of period feeds the other. application
shows what the abstractions got wrong or left out; abstraction gives the next
round of application something better to work with. the meta domain was
written in one abstract stretch, and the work then turned back to strands,
because strands were what mattered next.

it's the idea of teaching both sides at once (see [pedagogy](pedagogy.md)) on
another axis. there, the pure and the applied form are held together in one
lesson; here, they take turns, across periods of work.

**when to switch.** abstraction stops paying when its questions keep leading
to more questions without bound, and nothing could be built from the answers
yet. application
stops paying when it keeps running into the same unexamined assumption. both
are signs that it's time for the other kind of period.

**setting aside isn't losing.** a parked abstraction stays available: deferred
lists and rough notes keep it findable, with enough written down to pick it up
again (see "the life of an idea", above).

**the case against.** oscillation can hide avoidance: abstraction as a way of
never shipping, application as a way of never thinking. switching has a cost,
since each kind of period takes time to get back into. and some parked ideas
never come back, which may be right, or may be a loss nobody notices.

### two ideas, in turn

oscillation can also run between ideas rather than between levels: holding two
ideas, related or not, and moving attention back and forth between them over a
stretch of time. the hypothesis is that a disciplined version of this keeps
thinking from crystallizing too early. each switch loosens whatever the last
stretch had fixed in place, which helps against anchoring and blind spots, and
the switching acts as a kind of filter, jarring loose perspectives that don't
hold up. moving between ideas is usually criticized, and at some timescales
rightly so; the question is what makes it healthy.

it seems to depend on the timescale:

| timescale | for instance | what tends to happen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| seconds | flipping between tasks while working | mostly cost: time lost at each switch, and attention left behind on the last task |
| minutes, while reflecting | five to ten minutes on each of two ideas, for an hour or two, with nothing else asking for attention | possibly benefit: each idea rests while the other is held |
| days and weeks | a few projects kept going side by side | benefit: time away loosens fixed ideas, and the projects feed each other |

a likely mechanism is **incubation**: an idea set aside for a while often comes
back clearer, because the framing that had trapped it fades. holding two ideas
in turn gives each one an incubation while the other has the attention. and
when the two are unrelated, the switches bring together frames of reference
that would otherwise never meet, which is where new perspectives often come
from.

the conditions seem to matter as much as the timescale. the version measured in
minutes has been observed (see [personal](personal.md)) lying awake in the
dark, most often in the early morning, after waking a couple of hours early
with the mind rested, and sometimes at night, before sleep. little else
competes for attention then, and there's nothing to perform, only to think.

what seems to make it healthy rather than scattered:

- **it's chosen**: a deliberate alternation, not a drift between distractions
- **each turn goes somewhere**: long enough to think, not just to glance
- **the number is small**: two, perhaps a few; past some point it breaks down
- **a note at each switch**, when the stretch is long: where the idea was, and
  what comes next
- **a question on return**: what does the other idea say about this one?

**in these docs.** switching from strands to the meta domain, and back, is what
loosened the old framing of the strand domain: it came back seen as one
perspective, the spacer, on something deeper.

**the case against.** some problems need long, unbroken immersion, and
switching would break it. switching can disguise avoidance. and this rests on
one person's observation and on research into neighbouring practices, not on
studies of this one.

### micro and macro, in turn

one more axis: moving between the small structure of the work and the large.
in documents, the small structure is how a section opens, when a term is
bolded, how examples are laid out; the large is how chapters and domains fit
together. working on one sharpens the other: a pattern settled in one chapter
gets checked across all of them, and a change to the whole shows which small
patterns no longer fit. and switching scale is one of the easiest ways to keep
a piece of work from going stale.

### tangents, and coming back

the mechanics above are tools, not a formula, and following them too rigidly
kills what they're for. so the work is let go where it goes: a tangent with
energy behind it is followed, often far, because tangents are where many of the
best ideas turn up. what keeps this from becoming drift is the discipline to
come back: to return to the context the tangent left from, and resolve it, or
revisit it with what the tangent found. a tangent works like a call in a
program: the context it left is kept, and the work returns to it. the meta
domain grew out of a tangent from the strand work, and the work came back to
strands with a better framing of them.

**patience.** some of this takes years, and some of it decades. ideas are
iterated on with passion over long stretches, not forced to a finish by a
date. a hunch can sit for a long time before it's ready; the work is to keep
it alive, and keep coming back to it.

### tying off

alongside the patience, a piece of work is regularly **tied off**: brought into
a meaningful, self-contained chunk, even when it isn't fully realized. that
isn't the same as finishing (see "engagement first", above). a tied-off piece
may still be open at its edges, but it holds together on its own, it's
thoughtful and valuable as it stands, and it can be drawn on and referred to
later, by others or by a later self. a frozen draft, a change kept with what it
did and why, a chapter with its open questions marked: each is a piece tied
off.

**the case against.** a tangent can swallow the work it came from, and the way
back can be lost. patience can become an excuse for never shipping. and a
tied-off piece can be mistaken for a finished one. so the context left behind
is noted when a tangent starts, coming back is a habit rather than a hope, and
a tied-off piece says what's still open.

## related

explained changes; literate programming, where the explanation and the work
are written together; exploration and exploitation (James March), and doing
both by alternating over time, sometimes called temporal ambidexterity; Kolb's
learning cycle, from concrete experience to abstract ideas and back; divergent
and convergent thinking (J. P. Guilford), and the Double Diamond, in design;
Pasteur's quadrant (Donald Stokes), research driven by understanding and use
at once; incubation (Graham Wallas, and later Sio and Ormerod), and mind
wandering during incubation (Baird and colleagues); fixation and the
Einstellung effect (Abraham Luchins); bisociation (Arthur Koestler, *The Act of
Creation*); sleep and insight (Wagner and colleagues), and the edge of sleep
(Lacaux and colleagues); segmented sleep, a first and second sleep with a
waking spell between, common before artificial light (Roger Ekirch); a network
of enterprise (Howard Gruber); slow-motion multitasking (Tim Harford);
task-switching costs (Stephen Monsell) and attention residue (Sophie Leroy);
Richard Feynman's dozen favourite problems; flow (Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi);
self-determination theory (Edward Deci and Richard Ryan); sustainable pace, a
principle of agile software development; deliberate practice (K. Anders
Ericsson); burnout (Christina Maslach); micro and macro readings (Edward
Tufte); attention restoration (Rachel and Stephen Kaplan); slow hunches and
the adjacent possible (Steven Johnson); the commonplace book; the Zettelkasten,
a slip box of small, self-contained notes drawn on for decades (Niklas

(Eric Raymond); and yak shaving, the usual warning about tangents.
